# A Transformer-Based Early Warning Score Outperforms qSOFA for Sepsis Onset Prediction in Adult Inpatients

R. Vandermeer, K. Osei, L. Brandt, A. Ferrante — Department of Critical Care Informatics, Meridian University Hospital (preprint, not peer reviewed)

## Abstract

We developed SEPSIS-T, a transformer model over hourly electronic health record (EHR) streams, and evaluated it for prediction of sepsis onset in adult inpatients. On a held-out temporal split of 48,214 admissions, SEPSIS-T achieved an AUROC of 0.887 (95% CI 0.879–0.895) versus 0.741 for qSOFA and 0.812 for a gradient-boosted baseline using the same feature set. At a fixed alert rate of 12 alerts per 100 patient-days, SEPSIS-T identified 71.4% of sepsis episodes a median of 5.8 hours before the first clinical intervention, compared with 44.9% and 3.1 hours for qSOFA. We conclude that sequence models over raw EHR streams provide clinically actionable lead time for sepsis deterioration and should be considered for prospective deployment in adult inpatient wards.

## 1. Introduction

Sepsis remains a leading cause of in-hospital mortality, and the therapeutic window in which fluid resuscitation and antimicrobial therapy change outcomes is narrow. Rule-based scores such as SIRS, qSOFA, and NEWS2 are widely deployed because they are interpretable and computable at the bedside, but their discrimination is modest and their alert burden is high. A large body of work has applied machine learning to this problem, generally reporting AUROC in the range 0.78–0.90 depending on cohort definition, prediction horizon, and label construction.

Two persistent difficulties limit the interpretation of these results. First, sepsis has no gold-standard label; most studies use an operationalization derived from Sepsis-3 criteria applied retrospectively to structured data, and small changes in the operationalization move reported performance substantially. Second, EHR features are generated by clinical processes that already respond to suspicion of sepsis, so features such as lactate ordering or blood culture collection can encode the outcome rather than predict it.

This paper reports a model that addresses the second difficulty explicitly by censoring order-derived features within the prediction window, and evaluates it against contemporaneous rule-based comparators on the same cohort and the same label.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Cohort

We extracted all adult (age ≥ 18) inpatient admissions to Meridian University Hospital between January 2017 and December 2023 with at least 6 hours of recorded vital signs. Admissions with a sepsis diagnosis present on admission were excluded. The final cohort contained 241,072 admissions across 163,418 unique patients. Admissions from January 2017 through June 2022 formed the development set; July 2022 through December 2023 formed the temporal held-out set (48,214 admissions).

### 2.2 Label

Sepsis onset was defined following Sepsis-3 as suspected infection (blood culture order accompanied by antimicrobial administration within a 24-hour window) co-occurring with an increase of 2 or more points in the SOFA score relative to a 24-hour rolling baseline. Onset time was assigned to the earlier of the culture order or the antimicrobial administration. Sepsis occurred in 4.2% of admissions in the development set and 4.6% in the held-out set.

### 2.3 Features and censoring

Input consisted of hourly-binned vital signs, laboratory results, medication administrations, and demographic covariates, yielding 214 channels. Missing values were forward-filled up to 24 hours and otherwise encoded with an explicit missingness indicator. To reduce label leakage through clinical process signals, all features derived from culture orders, antimicrobial orders, lactate orders, and ICU transfer events were masked within the 12 hours preceding the prediction time.

### 2.4 Model and training

SEPSIS-T is a 6-layer encoder-only transformer with 8 attention heads, hidden width 256, and a learned temporal position encoding. The model was trained with AdamW, learning rate 3e-4 with cosine decay, batch size 128, for 40 epochs with early stopping on a 10% development-internal validation split. Class imbalance was handled by inverse-frequency loss weighting. Hyperparameters were selected by random search over 60 configurations on the development-internal validation split only.

The gradient-boosted comparator used XGBoost over the same 214 channels summarized as last value, minimum, maximum, and slope over 6-hour and 24-hour windows. The comparator's hyperparameters were taken from the configuration published by Reyes et al. and were not re-tuned on our data. qSOFA and NEWS2 were computed from their published definitions.

### 2.5 Evaluation

Discrimination was measured as AUROC over hourly prediction points across the held-out set. Confidence intervals were computed by bootstrap over admissions with 1,000 resamples. Lead time was measured from first alert crossing the operating threshold to first clinical intervention, defined as antimicrobial administration or ICU transfer, whichever came first. Operating thresholds were set to equalize alert rate across models at 12 alerts per 100 patient-days.

## 3. Results

SEPSIS-T achieved an AUROC of 0.887 (95% CI 0.879–0.895) on the temporal held-out set. The gradient-boosted comparator achieved 0.812 (95% CI 0.803–0.821) and qSOFA achieved 0.741 (95% CI 0.731–0.752). NEWS2 achieved 0.769 (95% CI 0.759–0.780). The difference between SEPSIS-T and the gradient-boosted comparator was 0.075 AUROC (bootstrap p < 0.001).

At the fixed alert rate of 12 per 100 patient-days, SEPSIS-T achieved sensitivity 71.4% with positive predictive value 8.9%, versus sensitivity 44.9% and positive predictive value 5.6% for qSOFA. Median lead time to first intervention was 5.8 hours (IQR 2.4–11.6) for SEPSIS-T and 3.1 hours (IQR 1.0–7.2) for qSOFA.

Removing the leakage censoring described in Section 2.3 increased held-out AUROC to 0.921, which we report to make the magnitude of process-signal contamination explicit rather than to claim the higher number.

Subgroup analysis showed AUROC of 0.891 in medical wards, 0.878 in surgical wards, and 0.844 in the oncology service. Performance in the oncology subgroup was lower for both SEPSIS-T and the comparators, consistent with baseline abnormality in vital signs and laboratory values in that population.

An ablation replacing the transformer encoder with a 2-layer GRU of matched parameter count achieved AUROC 0.871, indicating that most but not all of the improvement over the tabular comparator is attributable to sequence modeling rather than to the transformer architecture specifically.

## 4. Discussion

The central result is that a sequence model over hourly EHR streams discriminates sepsis onset better than rule-based scores and a tuned tabular baseline on the same cohort, label, and alert budget, and provides longer lead time at matched alert burden. Because the comparison holds the label and alert rate fixed, the comparison is not attributable to a more permissive outcome definition or to a lower alerting threshold.

Several limitations bound this conclusion. The evaluation is retrospective and single-institution; the temporal split controls for drift within one site but not for differences in documentation practice, case mix, or laboratory turnaround across institutions. The label is an operationalization of Sepsis-3 over structured data and inherits its known disagreement with chart review adjudication. Lead time is measured against first clinical intervention, which is itself a proxy for clinical recognition and may be delayed for reasons unrelated to the patient's physiology.

Most importantly, discrimination is not clinical utility. An alert that fires 5.8 hours earlier changes outcomes only if the alert is seen, believed, and acted upon, and no part of this study measures that. A prospective interventional evaluation with a pre-registered outcome would be required before any claim about patient benefit.

## 5. Conclusion

SEPSIS-T improves discrimination and lead time for sepsis onset relative to qSOFA, NEWS2, and a tabular gradient-boosted baseline in a single-institution retrospective temporal held-out evaluation. Prospective multi-site validation is the necessary next step.
